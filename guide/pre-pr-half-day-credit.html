The Pre-PR Half-Day Credit, In Detail

How students and work permit holders shave 12 to 18 months off their wait

Guides » Pre-PR Half-Day Credit

Last updated: 5 May 2026

The half-day credit is the rule that lets former international students, work permit holders, and protected persons apply for Canadian citizenship sooner than they otherwise could. It is also the rule that most applicants get wrong, partly because the math feels weird and partly because the cap surprises them.

This guide walks through who qualifies, how the credit is calculated, where the cap kicks in, and what happens at the edges.

What the rule actually says

For each day you were physically present in Canada as a temporary resident or protected person before becoming a permanent resident, you earn half a day of credit toward your 1,095-day citizenship requirement. The total credit you can earn this way is capped at 365 days.

That is the entire rule, in one sentence. Everything below is about the words inside it.

"Temporary resident" for this rule

A temporary resident, for the purpose of the half-day credit, is anyone who was lawfully in Canada under a temporary status during the relevant five-year window. In practice, this is:

  • International students on a valid study permit
  • Post-graduation work permit (PGWP) holders
  • Closed work permit holders (work permit tied to a specific employer)
  • Open work permit holders (including spouses of students or workers, with their own permit)
  • International Mobility Program participants, including IEC, NAFTA, CUSMA, and intra-company transferees
  • Visitors with a valid visitor record, super visa, or temporary resident permit

The word that does the work in that list is "lawfully." If you were in Canada without status, the time does not count.

"Protected person" for this rule

A protected person is someone who has been determined to be a Convention refugee or a person in need of protection under Canadian law. Time as a refugee claimant, before that determination is made, generally does not count. The clock for half-day credit starts on the date the protected person status is officially conferred.

How the half is actually counted

This is where people stumble. The rule does not mean that you literally use half of your physical days. It means each day produces 0.5 days of credit, summed across the eligible period, with a cap.

A worked example.

You arrived in Canada as a master's student on 1 September 2021. You finished the degree on 30 April 2023, then worked on a PGWP through 31 December 2023 when your PR was granted on 1 January 2024.

That is 1 September 2021 through 31 December 2023, which is 853 days. Assume you took two trips home during that period totalling 30 days abroad, so 823 physical days in Canada.

Half of 823 is 411.5 days of raw credit. The cap is 365 days, so your final pre-PR credit is 365 days. Anything beyond 730 calendar days of presence is wasted from a counting perspective, although it still strengthens your story for the actual application.

From your PR date forward, every day in Canada is one full day. To reach 1,095 days, you need 1,095 minus 365 equals 730 more days of physical presence after PR. If you do not leave Canada, that is roughly two years.

So in this scenario, you become eligible to apply about two years after your PR date, instead of the three years that someone with no pre-PR time would need.

Where the 365-day cap stops mattering

If you have less than 730 days of physical pre-PR presence, the cap is not your problem. Your half-day credit will simply be half of however many days you were actually here.

If you have more than 730 days of physical pre-PR presence, the cap will bind. Every additional day of pre-PR life in Canada beyond that point produces zero additional credit. You can still benefit by using those extra months for a stronger work history or stronger language acquisition, but they do not move your day count.

What about time abroad during the pre-PR period?

Days you spent outside Canada during your pre-PR period do not count, in exactly the same way they do not count after PR. If you went home for the summer between school years, those weeks are absent days and they are subtracted before the half is applied.

The departure and return days are still days in Canada. The full days in the middle are not.

What about time on a tourist visa?

A tourist visa, also called a temporary resident visa, gives you visitor status. If you held that status lawfully and were physically present in Canada during the five-year window, those days do qualify for the half-day credit. People often skip these days because they feel small. If you spent two summers in Canada visiting before you applied for a study permit, that may be 60 to 90 days of presence, which is 30 to 45 days of credit, which can shift your eligibility date by more than a month.

What about time without status?

This is the most common disqualifier. If your study permit expired and you stayed in Canada without status, even briefly, those days do not count. If your work permit was being renewed and you were on implied status, the implied status period does count, because implied status is a lawful status. The line between "implied status" and "no status" is technical, and if your file has any of these gaps, get a consultant to look at them before you submit.

What about time in a Canadian prison?

Time spent serving a sentence in a Canadian prison, jail, penitentiary, or reformatory does not count toward physical presence. Time on parole, probation, or release from custody can also be problematic. If this applies to you, do not rely on a calculator. Talk to a lawyer.

A small note on "the day you became a PR"

The COPR date, the date you actually landed and were granted PR, is the dividing line between half-day and full-day counting. The PR card issue date is later and is not what matters here. If you landed on 1 January 2024, your half-day credit covers 31 December 2023 and earlier. From 1 January 2024 forward, days are full days.

Bringing it together

The half-day credit is generous. For students and work permit holders, it routinely shaves 12 to 18 months off the wait. The cap and the rolling five-year window make the timing nontrivial, which is why a calculator is useful before you apply.

When you are ready to submit, run the numbers through the official IRCC physical presence calculator at canada.ca. The version on this site is for planning. The IRCC version is for filing.
